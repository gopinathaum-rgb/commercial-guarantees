A milestone becomes commercially interesting when something depends on it.

For example:

> "Milestone payment is due when the implementation is complete."

The phrase "complete" may be obvious during a smooth project.

It becomes much less obvious when the vendor and customer disagree.

## The commercial chain

A consequential milestone can be made more explicit:

| Question | Example |
|---|---|
| What is promised? | Production deployment |
| What counts as complete? | Deployment + mandatory acceptance tests |
| What evidence proves it? | Deployment record + test results + production verification |
| Who verifies it? | Agreed verification authority/process |
| What happens if it cannot be demonstrated? | Predefined commercial consequence |

The purpose is not to eliminate commercial judgment.

It is to make the factual basis for that judgment clearer.

## What happens when the definition is weak?

A disputed milestone may lead to:

- payment being delayed
- rework
- escalation
- negotiation
- additional project-management effort
- arguments over emails and meeting notes
- uncertainty about which party bears the cost

The current research question is narrower:

> **Can a specific high-consequence commitment be defined so that its completion can later be established from agreed evidence?**

## Questions for implementation and commercial teams

If you have seen this happen, the useful questions are:

1. What was the milestone?
2. What did each side believe "complete" meant?
3. What evidence existed?
4. Who made the final determination?
5. Was money delayed or withheld?
6. What did the organization do afterward to prevent a recurrence?

The last question matters because the workaround itself reveals the economic cost of ambiguity.

## Can You Prove It?

A real, redacted commitment is the best test.

The current workflow is a private manual audit of one commitment:

**Commitment → Completion condition → Evidence → Verification → Commercial consequence → Reconstruction**
